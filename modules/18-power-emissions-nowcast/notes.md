# Notes: what I tried, what I threw away, what I got wrong

**Summing the 27 member states instead of using Ember's EU aggregate.** The estimand says EU27, and my first instinct was to build it from the member series so that it was defined by me, not by Ember. The member series start in 2015 (most states), 2016 (Italy, Slovenia), 2017 (Malta), 2018 (Luxembourg) and December 2018 (Croatia), so a sum is complete from 2019 only, and the 2018 backtest year would have no history. I used the aggregate, which starts in January 2016, and checked it against the sum where all 27 exist (largest monthly gap 0.43% for fossil, 0.41% for total). The cost is two years of history for 2018 and three for 2019, which I state as a limit.

**The regression, and the fit that cannot separate its sources.** My first synthetic test gave every source the same seasonal shape and level path, and the regression returned nonsense, because the regressors were perfectly collinear. The real data do the same thing more gently: fitted on 2016 and 2017 the factors for bioenergy and wind are −0.005 and 0.034 where Ember's own are 0.230 and 0.013. I kept the nine-source form as the base because I had built it before looking at the sweep, then ran fossil-only and two-group versions as variants. Fossil-only is slightly better at 9 months (2.88% against 2.95%) and the two-group form is worse (3.45%); I did not switch the base after seeing that. The regression is worse than the share rule in 13 of 14 variants at three and six months and in all 14 at nine and ten, so the question is not close.

**Interval: empirical quantiles, then Student t.** With eight errors per method, the min and max of the errors cover a new draw with probability 8/9 at best, and a 95% empirical quantile does not exist. I used the RMS (not the standard deviation, so bias counts against the method) with a t quantile on 8 degrees of freedom, and added a leave-one-year-out coverage check. I considered correcting each method for its mean error and dropped it: the mean is estimated from the same eight points. The check came out at 88% for last year, 97% for the share rule and 100% for the regression; with eight years per cell it is coarse, and I say so in the README instead of reading it as confirmation.

**Bootstrap over years, paired.** I first thought of bootstrapping the months. A year's shock hits every method and every horizon together, so the unit is the year, drawn once for all columns. A test builds identical methods and requires identical RMSE draws.

**Selection bias.** The headline uses the method with the lowest backtest RMSE, which is selection on the same errors that set the interval. I added a leave-one-year-out selection (pick on seven years, score on the eighth). It picks the share rule every time and gives the same RMSE, so the bias is zero here. It would not be zero with closer methods.

**A tenth month.** The data have October 2026. The roadmap asks for 3, 6 and 9 months, so the headline is at nine; I added 10 as an extra horizon rather than quietly using the newest month. It narrows the interval to [573, 621] and excludes 2025's value, which the nine-month interval does not. I would not call that a finding about 2026: it is a property of one more month of data.

**Which inventory line to validate against.** I looked at CRF 1.A.1 (all energy industries, including refining) and 1.A.1.a (public electricity and heat production) and used 1.A.1.a, the closer match to power generation. Ember's fossil emissions are 99.3% of it on average. I did not keep the exploratory numbers for 1.A.1. This check covers 2016 to 2024 and cannot test a nowcast.

**Ember's yearly file for a longer history.** Ember publishes annual data back to 2000, which would lengthen the history for the level. The share-of-year method needs monthly data, so it would not have fed any benchmark here. I did not download it.

**A threshold I set wrong.** The test of a mix shift (coal doubling in the nowcast year) first required the share rule to be wrong by more than 2%. It is wrong by 1.3% on my synthetic mix, so I lowered the threshold to 0.5%. The test asserts that the regression is exact and the share rule is not; the size of the gap depends on the synthetic design and carries no empirical meaning.

**What I would check next.** The vintage problem: archive Ember's file every month and replay the nowcast on the file as it stood in each month, so that revisions enter the error. That is the one thing that could make the measured error too small, and one extraction cannot show it.
